\begin{abstract}
Model-Based Diffusion (MBD) solves optimal control problems by annealed,
sampling-based score ascent, but it evaluates its rollout model thousands
of times per control step, restricting it to platforms where a physics
simulation is affordable at the control rate. We propose \bkmbd{}, which
replaces the simulator rollout with a learned \emph{bilinear} deep
Koopman surrogate, augmented by a quantile-fitted model-error tube, at a
per-step cost fixed by the lifting dimension. The bilinear form is not
incidental: when the input reaches the tracked output through a
configuration-dependent gain---a vehicle's heading, a manipulator's
Jacobian---we show that the constant input map of a \emph{linear}
Koopman model incurs an irreducible one-step error equal to the
dispersion of that gain. Closed-loop experiments on SE(2) parking and on
end-effector reaching with a kinematic $7$-DOF arm and a physics-simulated
Franka FR3 confirm the analysis in both directions: the linear rollout
fails or stalls ($0/20$, $0/35$, and $7/35$ strict on the FR3), whereas
the bilinear rollout matches the true-dynamics oracle in all $90$ trials
while reducing the FR3 planning step from $4.1$\,s to $64$\,ms, within
$1.3\times$ of the control period; an optimizer ablation attributes the
final-precision gains to the annealed schedule itself.
\end{abstract}

Koopman operator, sampling-based control, model-based diffusion, data-driven

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%==============================================================
\IEEEPARstart{S}{ampling-based} optimal control has become a workhorse for
robotic systems with complex nonlinear dynamics. Instead of solving a
deterministic program at every step, controllers such as Model Predictive
Path Integral (MPPI) control~\cite{kappen2005, theodorou2010,
williams2017mppi, williams2016aggressive} and, more recently, Model-Based
Diffusion (MBD)~\cite{pan2024model, xue2025full} evaluate hundreds of
sampled control sequences by forward simulation and update the nominal
sequence by a cost-weighted average.
MBD reinterprets this population-and-reweight primitive as one step of
reverse diffusion: the weighted mean is a Monte-Carlo estimate of the
score of a Gaussian-smoothed target
distribution~\cite{song2019generative, song2020score}, and repeating it
along a decreasing noise schedule yields an annealed, coarse-to-fine
search. This
derivative-free structure places essentially no requirement on the
dynamics or the cost, and parallelizes trivially on modern hardware. Its
price, however, is easy to overlook: the \emph{rollout model} is evaluated
thousands of times per control step, so it dominates both the
computational cost and the achievable control rate. MBD in particular
inherits its rollouts from a physics simulator~\cite{pan2024model}, which
confines it to offline trajectory optimization or to platforms where a
full simulation step is affordable at the sampling rate.

The Koopman operator~\cite{koopman1931, mezic2005} offers a principled way
to remove this bottleneck. By lifting the state through a dictionary of
observables---classically hand-designed~\cite{williams2015edmd,
proctor2016dmdc}, and more recently learned by deep
networks~\cite{lusch2018deep}---the nonlinear dynamics become (near-)linear
in the lifted space, so a rollout reduces to a sequence of matrix--vector
products whose cost is fixed by the lifting dimension, \emph{independent of
the complexity of the underlying dynamics}. \emph{Linear} lifted predictors
dominate this literature: they keep Koopman MPC convex by
construction~\cite{korda2018mpc, strasser2026}, and a learned linear deep
Koopman model has recently been coupled with MPPI, reporting control
performance close to true-dynamics MPPI at a fraction of the
cost~\cite{mppidk2026}.

The choice of a linear lift, however, carries a structural restriction. A
linear Koopman model lets the input act through a single constant matrix,
the same regardless of the robot's configuration. Yet for a large class of
robots the input does not act this way: a wheeled vehicle's speed command
moves it along its current heading; a manipulator's joint-velocity command
moves the end-effector through its configuration-dependent Jacobian. In both
cases the input reaches the tracked output through a
\emph{configuration-dependent gain} that no constant input matrix can
reproduce. Notably, in the experimental settings of the linear-lift
controllers above the input enters the learned dynamics
state-independently (body-frame force or acceleration inputs, with
the attitude kinematics integrated analytically), so this coupling never
has to be represented by the model. We show in Sec.~\ref{sec:method} that
the restriction is a model-class property---the best constant surrogate is
the \emph{mean} of the gain along the visited states, with an irreducible
one-step error equal to the gain's dispersion---and in Sec.~\ref{sec:exp}
that its closed-loop consequence is severe: the linear rollout succeeds in
$0$ of $20$ trials on an analytic SE(2) testbed and reaches the strict
tolerance in only $7$ of $35$ trials on a physics-simulated manipulator,
while succeeding only when the coupling is removed from the learning
problem by hand-supplied analytic structure, which the model-free setting
withholds.

For control-affine systems the exact Koopman form instead carries a
state-dependent input map~\cite{iacob2024}, whose smallest realization is
the bilinear class, and bilinear realizations have shown clear modeling
and control advantages~\cite{bruder2021bilinear, folkestad2021,
mamakoukas2019}---but within MPC, where the bilinear term destroys the
convexity that motivates the linear restriction in the first place.
Sampling-based control imposes no convexity requirement, so the
restriction is unnecessary: the bilinear model can simply become the
default rollout, at the same matrix--vector cost regime as the linear one.
Hence, in this paper, we propose \bkmbd{}, a sampling-based control
framework that embeds a learned \emph{bilinear} deep Koopman rollout
inside the annealed diffusion-style update of MBD. At every control step,
the current output is lifted once, all candidate control sequences are
propagated entirely in the lifted space by the bilinear recursion, and the
annealed update refines the nominal sequence over a decreasing noise
schedule before the first control is executed. A scalar error tube, fitted
to the model's one-step residuals in the spirit of proportional error
bounds for certified Koopman control~\cite{strasser2026, safedmd2024}, is
propagated along each candidate and added to the cost, discouraging the
search from regions where the surrogate is least trustworthy.
Unlike diffusion models used as \emph{learned} motion
priors~\cite{janner2022planning, chi2025diffusion, carvalho2023motion}, no
trajectory distribution is learned here: the diffusion machinery serves
purely as an optimizer, and the learning problem concerns the rollout
dynamics.

The contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{\bkmbd{}: MBD at the timescale of the control period.} We
embed a learned bilinear deep Koopman rollout, made model-error-aware by
a quantile-fitted error tube, inside the annealed MBD update; the lifted
rollout decouples the planning cost from the complexity of the true
dynamics---$64\times$ cheaper than the physics-engine oracle on the FR3,
turning a $4.1$\,s planning step into a $64$\,ms one at no loss in
final accuracy.
\item \textbf{Controlled closed-loop validation in both directions.}
Within a single MBD stack that varies only the rollout model, the linear
rollout fails or stalls ($0/20$ and $0/35$ on kinematic testbeds; $7/35$
strict on a physics-simulated Franka FR3), whereas \bkmbd{} matches the
true-dynamics oracle in all $90$ trials at oracle-level final errors with
no analytic model; a structure-informed linear baseline granted the
analytic coupling also succeeds ($90/90$), isolating the failure to the
model class and delimiting what the linear lift would require in
deployment.
\item \textbf{What the anneal contributes.} Holding the learned surrogate
fixed and varying only the update schedule, strict success climbs
monotonically from the single-stage MPPI operating point to the full
anneal ($25 \to 28 \to 32 \to 35$ of $35$), and a Gaussian-homotopy and
precision-floor analysis explains why: annealing both smooths away the
surrogate's fine-scale spurious minima early and removes the
Monte-Carlo noise floor of a fixed scale late
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:exp_anneal}).
\item \textbf{Representability analysis of the linear lift.} Supporting
the design, we trace the linear rollout's failure to a model-class
property: the best constant input map is the mean of the
configuration-dependent gain, with an irreducible one-step error equal
to the gain's dispersion, and the bilinear lift restores exactly the
structure the linear lift discards.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}\label{subsec:problem}

Consider the discrete-time system
\begin{equation}
s_{t+1} = f(s_t, u_t), \qquad
s_t \in \mathcal{X} \subset \mathbb{R}^{n},\quad
u_t \in \mathcal{U} = [\underline{u}, \bar{u}]^{m},
\label{eq:sys}
\end{equation}
in the \emph{model-free, real-time} setting: the dynamics $f$ are unknown or
too costly to integrate at the sampling rate, so that a true-dynamics
rollout is available only as an \emph{oracle} reference, not as a deployable
controller. Only interaction data
$\mathcal{D} = \{(s_i, u_i, s_i^{+})\}$ is available for model learning.

Each task defines observable features $b(s) \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ used both as
the Koopman training state and in the cost (unicycle:
$b = [x, y, \sin\theta, \cos\theta - 1]$; manipulator:
$b = [q, p_{\mathrm{tcp}}]$, joints and tool-center-point position). The controller operates in a receding
horizon: at state $s_t$ it seeks a control sequence
$U \in \mathbb{R}^{mT}$ minimizing
\begin{equation}
J(U; s_t) = \sum_{k=1}^{T} c\big(\hat{b}_k, u_{k-1}\big)
+ \phi\big(\hat{b}_T\big),
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
where $\hat b_k$ is produced by the rollout model under study, applies the
first control $u_0$ to \eqref{eq:sys}, and replans.

\subsection{Model-Based Diffusion: A Review}\label{subsec:mbd}

MBD~\cite{pan2024model} treats \eqref{eq:cost} as sampling from the
Boltzmann target
\begin{equation}
p_0(U) \;\propto\; \exp\!\big(-J(U)/\alpha\big)
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
and descends an annealing ladder of Gaussian-smoothed marginals
\begin{equation}
p_{\sigma}(U) = \big(p_0 * \varphi_{\sigma}\big)(U)
= \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^{2} I)}
\big[\, p_0(U - \varepsilon) \,\big],
\label{eq:smooth}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_1 > \dots > \sigma_S$. By Tweedie's formula, the score of the
smoothed target is a denoising mean shift,
\begin{equation}
\nabla_U \log p_{\sigma}(U)
= \frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\,U_0 \mid U\,\big] - U}{\sigma^{2}} .
\label{eq:tweedie}
\end{equation}
Because the smoothing kernel $\varphi_\sigma(U - U')$ is itself the density
of the proposal $U' \sim \mathcal{N}(U, \sigma^{2} I)$, the posterior mean
in \eqref{eq:tweedie} is a ratio of expectations under the proposal, and
with $N$ candidates $U_k = U + \sigma \varepsilon_k$,
$\varepsilon_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, projected onto $\mathcal{U}^{T}$,
the self-normalized estimator is the cost-weighted mean
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}\big[U_0 \mid U\big] = \sum_{k=1}^{N} w_k U_k,
\quad
w_k = \frac{e^{-(J(U_k) - J_{\min})/\alpha}}
           {\sum_{j} e^{-(J(U_j) - J_{\min})/\alpha}} .
\label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}
Under the box projection the candidates are no longer exactly Gaussian
about $U$, so \eqref{eq:weights} estimates the score of the
correspondingly projected smoothing rather than \eqref{eq:smooth} itself;
every compared method shares the identical projection.
The update at stage $s$ is a preconditioned score step with optional
Langevin noise, \emph{with the step size tied to the noise level}:
\begin{equation}
U \leftarrow U + \eta_s\, \widehat{\nabla_U \log p_{\sigma_s}}(U)
+ \sqrt{2 \eta_s}\, \xi,
\qquad
\eta_s = \eta\, \sigma_s^{2} .
\label{eq:update}
\end{equation}
With $\eta = 1$ and no Langevin noise, \eqref{eq:update} reduces exactly to
the weighted-mean update $U \leftarrow \sum_k w_k U_k$.

\section{Proposed Method}\label{sec:method}
%==============================================================

\subsection{Deep Koopman Lifting and Rollout Models}\label{subsec:models}

All learned rollout models share a deep lift with an exact-decoder
convention. With $d = \dim b$,
\begin{equation}
z = \Psi_\theta(b) =
\begin{bmatrix} b \\ \psi_\theta\big(\tau(b)\big) \end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^{r},
\qquad
C z = z_{1:d} = b,
\label{eq:lift}
\end{equation}
where $\psi_\theta$ is a small $\tanh$ network and $\tau$ a fixed task
input map (unicycle: $\tau = \mathrm{id}$, $r = 8$; FR3:
$\tau(b) = [\sin q, \cos q]$, $r = 20$). Slicing the decoder avoids
learning a fragile reconstruction and ties the first $d$ lifted coordinates
to the physical output. Two lifted dynamics classes are compared:
\begin{align}
\textbf{linear (DK):}\quad
& z^{+} = A z + B u ,
\label{eq:lin}\\[2pt]
\textbf{bilinear (BK):}\quad
& z^{+} = A z + B_0 u + \sum_{i=1}^{m} u_i B_i z ,
\label{eq:bil}
\end{align}
where \eqref{eq:bil} is compactly written as
$z^{+} = M(u)\, z + B_0 u$ with the input-dependent transition matrix
$M(u) = A + \sum_i u_i B_i$. For control-affine systems the exact Koopman
form carries a state-dependent input map~\cite{iacob2024}; the bilinear
class is its smallest realization, restricting that dependence to be
linear in the lifted state: its input tangent map $\partial z^{+} / \partial u_i = B_0 e_i + B_i z$ depends on the
state through $B_i z$, whereas the linear form's
$\partial z^{+}/\partial u = B$ is constant. Both classes roll out in the
same matrix--vector regime; at planning time the current output is lifted
once, $z_0 = \Psi_\theta(b(s_t))$, and every candidate is propagated
entirely in the lifted space.

\subsection{Why a Constant Input Map Cannot Represent the Coupling}\label{subsec:representability}

Let the tracked output increment be first-order in the input,
$\Delta y = \Phi(s)\, u\, \Delta t + O(\Delta t^{2})$, with
configuration-dependent gain $\Phi(s)$ (unicycle: the heading rotation;
manipulator: the Jacobian $J(q)$ on the end-effector rows). In the linear lift the
input reaches the output only through constant matrices: the one-step map
is $CB$, and every input-to-output Markov parameter $C A^{k} B$ is
state-independent, so the entire input--output response of \eqref{eq:lin}
is linear time-invariant regardless of the lifted drift.

\begin{proposition}[Irreducible error of a constant gain]
\label{prop:const}
Let inputs satisfy $\mathbb{E}[u u^{\top}] = \lambda I$ independently of
the state $s \sim \rho$. Then
\begin{equation}
\arg\min_{X}\, \mathbb{E}_{s,u}\big\|(\Phi(s) - X) u\big\|^{2}
= \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \rho}\big[\Phi(s)\big],
\label{eq:meangain}
\end{equation}
and the minimum equals
$\lambda\, \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \rho}\big\| \Phi(s) -
\mathbb{E}[\Phi] \big\|_{F}^{2}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\mathbb{E}_u \|(\Phi - X) u\|^{2}
= \operatorname{tr}\big((\Phi - X)\, \mathbb{E}[u u^{\top}]\,
(\Phi - X)^{\top}\big) = \lambda \|\Phi(s) - X\|_{F}^{2}$; minimizing its
expectation over $s$, a quadratic in $X$, gives the mean, with the variance
as the residual.
\end{proof}

The best constant surrogate is the \emph{mean gain}, and the irreducible
error equals the \emph{dispersion of the gain along the visited
distribution}---a model-class property that no amount of training can
remove. The unicycle is the extreme case: parking sweeps the heading over
the full circle, $\mathbb{E}[(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)] \approx 0$, so the
optimal constant input channel for the speed command is \emph{zero}---the
model discards the actuator. The bilinear class instead realizes the
state-dependent gain $u \mapsto C\big(\sum_i u_i B_i z\big)$, whose columns
$C B_i z$ are linear in $z$: the heading rotation is exactly expressible
since $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$ are (affine) coordinates of $z$, and
the manipulator Jacobian, whose entries are multilinear in
$\{\sin q_i, \cos q_i\}$ across joints, is expressible once the learned
features supply the cross-products. We emphasize the scope of this
analysis: Proposition~\ref{prop:const} is a representability statement
about the one-step, first-order input channel under the stated excitation,
not a closed-loop theorem. How this representability gap is amplified into
closed-loop failure once an optimizer selects controls through the
model is traced empirically in Sec.~\ref{subsec:exp_mechanism}.

\subsection{The Structure-Informed Alternative and Its Cost}\label{subsec:split}

Proposition~\ref{prop:const} indicates the one repair available to the
linear class: remove the coupling from the learning problem. We formalize
this as the \dksplit{} baseline, which mirrors the experimental structure
of linear-lift controllers~\cite{mppidk2026, korda2018mpc}: for the
unicycle, a linear map $G$ is fitted by least squares from the input to
the \emph{body-frame} displacement,
$[\Delta p_{\mathrm{body}}; \Delta\theta] = G u$ with
$\Delta p_{\mathrm{body}} = R(-\theta_k)(p_{k+1} - p_k)$, and rollouts
compose the prediction analytically with the current heading (an $SE(2)$
composition); for the manipulator, a linear deep Koopman model is trained
on the joint state only, and the analytic forward kinematics is applied to
the decoded joints when evaluating the cost. In both cases the input then
enters the \emph{learned} part state-independently, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:const} no longer binds. \dksplit{} is the fair
representative of what linear-lift control actually deploys---and its
requirement is exactly the point: the analytic rotation or forward
kinematics that the model-free setting withholds. The bilinear rollout
\eqref{eq:bil} needs neither.

\subsection{Multi-Step Training}\label{subsec:training}

All learned models are trained on random-rollout snippets by the same
$H$-step loss ($H = 15$),
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{H} \sum_{h=1}^{H}
\Big[ \big\| C \hat{z}_h - b_h \big\|^{2}
+ \gamma \big\| \hat{z}_h - \Psi_\theta(b_h)^{\mathrm{sg}} \big\|^{2}
\Big],
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
with $\hat z_0 = \Psi_\theta(b_0)$, $\hat z_{h+1}$ given by
\eqref{eq:lin} or \eqref{eq:bil}, $\gamma = 0.1$, and stop-gradient
($\mathrm{sg}$) on the latent target. The bilinear input matrices are
\emph{zero-initialized}, $B_i = 0$, so that training starts exactly at the
linear model and adds bilinear structure only where the data demands it;
this stabilizes the $m$ per-channel matrices on the 7-DOF manipulator.
Datasets are generated once and shared across every learned model and
training seed, so that the compared classes differ in nothing but the
lifted dynamics \eqref{eq:lin} vs.\ \eqref{eq:bil}. One-step regression in
place of \eqref{eq:loss} yields rollouts that diverge before the planning
horizon.

\subsection{Model-Error Tube}\label{subsec:tube}

To make the planner aware of where the surrogate can be trusted, the
one-step lifted residuals on the training set,
$r_k = \Psi_\theta(b_{k+1}) - \big(M(u_k)\Psi_\theta(b_k) + B_0 u_k\big)$,
are covered by a proportional bound
$\|r_k\| \le c_x \|z_k\| + c_u \|u_k\|$ fitted at quantile coverage: a
least-squares fit of $(\hat c_x, \hat c_u)$ to the residual norms is
rescaled by the empirical $(1-\delta)$-quantile of the coverage ratios,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s_\delta &= Q_{1-\delta}\!\left[
\frac{\|r_k\|}{\hat c_x \|z_k\| + \hat c_u \|u_k\|}
\right], \\
(c_x, c_u) &= \max(s_\delta, 1) \,(\hat c_x, \hat c_u),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:tubefit}
\end{equation}
with $\delta = 10^{-3}$. Quantile coverage, rather than a worst-case fit
over every residual, keeps the constants proportional to the typical
model error; Sec.~\ref{subsec:exp_cost} quantifies how a worst-case fit
lets a few rest-to-motion transients dominate the planning cost.
During planning a scalar tube is propagated along each candidate with the
precomputed norm bound
$\bar m(u) = \|A\|_2 + \sum_i |u_i| \|B_i\|_2 \ge \|M(u)\|_2$:
\begin{equation}
e_{k+1} = \big(\bar m(u_k) + c_x\big) e_k
+ c_x \|\hat z_k\| + c_u \|u_k\|,
\qquad e_0 = 0 .
\label{eq:tube}
\end{equation}
Wherever the fitted bound holds, the recursion maintains
$e_k \ge \|\delta_k\|$ for the true-vs-predicted lifted error
$\delta_k = \Psi_\theta(b_k) - \hat z_k$, by induction on the exact error
dynamics $\delta_{k+1} = M(u_k)\delta_k + r_k$ of the bilinear rollout.
The planner then minimizes the tube-augmented cost
\begin{equation}
\tilde J(U) = J(U) + \beta_e \sum_{k} e_k ,
\label{eq:tubecost}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_e$ scales with the task cost magnitude; $\beta_e = 0$ recovers
plain \bkmbd{}, and the fitted constants are empirical proxies rather than
certified bounds.

In summary, one control step of \bkmbd{} consists of: lift the measured
output once by \eqref{eq:lift}; for each annealing stage
$s = 1, \dots, S$, sample $N$ candidate sequences, propagate them in the
lifted space by \eqref{eq:bil} while accumulating \eqref{eq:tube}, weight
them by \eqref{eq:weights} applied to \eqref{eq:tubecost}, and update the
nominal sequence by \eqref{eq:update}; execute the first control, warm-start
the next step by a one-step shift, and replan.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:exp}
%==============================================================
We evaluate the framework on two main testbeds, both first-order coupled
in the sense of Sec.~\ref{subsec:representability}: an SE(2) unicycle
parking task, where the coupling is exact, and end-effector reaching
with a Franka Research 3 (FR3) manipulator simulated with full
rigid-body physics in MuJoCo, whose joint-velocity servo realizes the
first-order interface only up to lag and load.
A third testbed, a kinematic $7$-DOF arm, carries the optimizer study of
Sec.~\ref{subsec:exp_anneal}. The
experiments are organized to support the claims of Sec.~\ref{sec:intro}:
the failure of the full-lift linear rollout and the oracle-level
performance of the bilinear rollout
(Secs.~\ref{subsec:exp_unicycle}--\ref{subsec:exp_franka}), the mechanism
by which a modest open-loop gap becomes that closed-loop failure once an
optimizer sits on top of the model (Sec.~\ref{subsec:exp_mechanism}),
what the annealed schedule itself contributes
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:exp_anneal}), the
rollout-cost decoupling together with an empirical validation of the tube
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:exp_cost}), and finally the boundary of the regime in
which the bilinear class is needed at all
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:exp_regime}).

\subsection{Protocol and Compared Methods}\label{subsec:setup}

All four planners run inside one implementation of the MBD optimizer of
Sec.~\ref{subsec:mbd}; the \emph{only} component that differs across
methods is the rollout model that maps a candidate control sequence to a
cost. \mbdtrue{} rolls out the true dynamics (the analytic model on the
unicycle, the MuJoCo simulator on the FR3) and serves as the oracle
upper bound. \dkmbd{} rolls out the full-lift linear deep Koopman model
\eqref{eq:lin}. \dksplit{} is the structure-informed control of
Sec.~\ref{subsec:split}: it is granted the analytic coupling (the SE(2)
group action on the unicycle, the forward kinematics on the FR3) and
learns only the input-affine remainder. \bkmbd{} rolls out the bilinear
model \eqref{eq:bil} with the tube cost \eqref{eq:tubecost}. The optimizer
uses $S = 5$ annealing stages, temperature $\alpha = 0.4$, and step scale
$\eta = 1$ (the deterministic weighted-mean limit of \eqref{eq:update});
per task we fix the sample count, noise schedule, horizon, and cost across
all methods (unicycle: $N = 768$, $\sigma\!: 0.9 \to 0.2$, $T = 40$;
FR3: $N = 800$, $\sigma\!: 1.2 \to 0.3$, $T = 15$).

The learned models follow one training recipe (Sec.~\ref{subsec:training}):
for each of five seeds, the linear, bilinear, and split models are trained
on the same dataset of $6{,}000$ random-excitation snippets of length $15$,
so that every learned-model comparison shares data, architecture, epochs,
and optimizer, and differs only in the lifted dynamics class. Every
(task, seed) pairing then runs all closed-loop cases from identical initial
sequences, giving $5 \times 4 = 20$ trials on the unicycle and
$5 \times 7 = 35$ trials on the FR3 (and likewise $35$ on the kinematic
arm of Sec.~\ref{subsec:exp_anneal}). We report the task success rate, the
strict success rate at a tighter tolerance, the final error, and the
planning time per control step.

\subsection{Unicycle Parking}\label{subsec:exp_unicycle}

lift is merely under-parametrized, and that a generic neural dynamics

fails to certify closed-loop precision

It also costs $2.7\times$ more per rollout and forgoes the lifted
linear-algebra structure on which the tube recursion~\eqref{eq:tube}
rests.
The bilinear lift is thus the option that pairs nonlinear-class
accuracy with matrix--vector cost, tube-compatible structure, and
oracle-level closed-loop precision.

\subsection{From Open-Loop Error to Closed-Loop Failure}
\label{subsec:exp_mechanism}

The closed-loop verdicts above raise a mechanism question: is the linear
lift failing because it is a much worse \emph{predictor}, or because of how
an \emph{optimizer} uses it? Because MBD interrogates the rollout model
$S \times N$ times per control step and executes whatever the model scores
best, the distinction matters, and it can be read off the open-loop error
of the very checkpoints used in closed loop.
Figure~\ref{fig:pred_horizon} shows the open-loop prediction error of the
FR3 checkpoints against the rollout horizon on held-out data, averaged
over the five training seeds and decomposed into the output rows that
carry the configuration-dependent coupling (TCP) and the joint rows,
where the input enters state-independently.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/pred_error_horizon.png}
\caption{Open-loop prediction error vs.\ horizon for the trained FR3
checkpoints used in closed loop (mean $\pm$ std over five seeds,
held-out data, log scale). The classes coincide on the joint rows
(dashed); on the TCP rows (solid) the linear model is only $1.33\times$
worse at the planning horizon $H = 15$---yet reaches the strict
tolerance in only $7/35$ closed-loop trials against $35/35$ for the
bilinear model.}
\label{fig:pred_horizon}
\end{figure}

Two observations localize the failure. First, the penalty is exactly where
Proposition~\ref{prop:const} predicts: on the joint rows the two classes
are indistinguishable, while on the coupled rows the linear model carries
a persistent excess error. Second, and more consequential, the open-loop
gap is \emph{modest}---$1.33\times$ at the planning horizon---because the
Jacobian along reaching motions has a non-zero mean, which the constant
input map of Proposition~\ref{prop:const} captures; the irreducible part
is only the \emph{dispersion} around that mean. Closed loop, this modest
gap becomes $7/35$ strict versus $35/35$. The amplification is a property
of optimizing through the model rather than predicting with it: the
residual left by the mean gain is a \emph{structural bias} that always
points the same way for a given configuration, the annealed search
selects the candidates whose predicted progress exploits this
bias, and receding-horizon replanning re-commits to the biased direction
at every step. The unicycle sits at the far end of the same spectrum:
parking sweeps the heading over the full circle, the mean gain is nearly
zero, the one-step open-loop gap is already $40\times$ ($0.082$ vs.\
$0.002$ RMSE), and the closed loop diverges outright. Open-loop error
therefore under-reports the model-class deficit in proportion to how
benign the visited gain distribution is---a rollout surrogate for a
sampling-based optimizer must be judged in closed loop, on the coupled
output rows, not by aggregate prediction scores.

\subsection{What the Anneal Contributes}\label{subsec:exp_anneal}

The optimizer itself is the remaining free variable: is the annealed,
multi-stage schedule of MBD doing work that a single-stage sampler---the
MPPI operating point---would not do with the same surrogate?
We answer on a third testbed on which the question turns out to be
sharpest: a kinematic $7$-DOF arm reaching end-effector targets under
joint-velocity inputs, with the same training recipe and protocol
($5$ seeds $\times$ $7$ targets, success below $5$\,cm, strict below
$2.5$\,cm), whose main comparison mirrors the FR3 pattern---the linear rollout fails outright ($0/35$,
final error $0.73 \pm 0.40$\,m) while \bkmbd{} matches the oracle
($35/35$, $0.024$ vs.\ $0.023$\,m).
Holding the trained bilinear checkpoints, task, cost, and tube fixed, we
then vary \emph{only} the update schedule (Table~\ref{tab:anneal}).

Strict success climbs monotonically as optimizer structure is added
($25 \to 28 \to 32 \to 35$), but only the full anneal reaches $35/35$,
at the same rollout budget as the fixed-noise five-stage variant (c);
five times more samples in a single stage (b) buys almost nothing.
Two mechanisms explain the ordering.
First, with a learned surrogate the evaluated cost is
$\hat J = J + e$, where the model-error term $e$ inherits fine-scale
structure from error accumulation along rollouts; each annealing stage
descends a Gaussian-smoothed landscape in which the Fourier components
of $e$ are attenuated by $e^{-\sigma^{2}\|\omega\|^{2}/2}$, so early
(large-$\sigma$) stages select the basin on a landscape whose spurious
model-induced minima are suppressed, and late stages refine where local
bias is small---one fixed scale must serve both roles at once.
Second, a fixed scale has a \emph{precision floor}: the weighted-mean
estimator carries Monte-Carlo noise of order
$\sigma/\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{eff}}}$ per axis
($N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 1/\sum_k w_k^{2}$), and in receding horizon the
control is executed after a single update, so this noise enters the
plant directly.
The failure signature confirms the mechanism: seven of the eight
fixed-noise failures across (a)--(c) are marginal stalls at
$0.050$--$0.079$\,m against the $0.05$\,m threshold---never
divergence---and annealing to $\sigma_S = 0.3$ removes every one.
On the FR3 the same sweep leaves all four configurations at $35/35$
strict ($17$--$64$\,ms per step): its $2.5$\,cm tolerance sits above the
floor there, so the schedule matters exactly when the required precision
approaches it---the regime a surrogate-based planner enters whenever
tolerances tighten.

\subsection{Rollout Cost and the Error Tube}\label{subsec:exp_cost}

The planning-time columns quantify the cost decoupling claimed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:intro}. A sampling planner spends
$S \times N \times T$ model evaluations per control step; for \bkmbd{}
each evaluation is one bilinear matrix--vector recursion in
$\mathbb{R}^{r}$, so its per-step cost is set by the lifting dimension
alone and is essentially constant across our testbeds ($64$--$95$\,ms),
while the oracle's cost tracks the complexity of the true dynamics---from
$81$\,ms on the analytic unicycle to $4.1$\,s on the physics engine, two
orders of magnitude the moment rollouts require a simulator.

The tube cost \eqref{eq:tubecost} acts as a soft trust region during the
anneal, and Fig.~\ref{fig:tube_val} checks empirically that the
quantile-fitted recursion still does the job it is asked to do. On
$2{,}000$ held-out FR3 rollouts of $40$ steps ($80{,}000$ propagated
steps, $2.7\times$ the planning horizon), the tube bounds the true lifted
error in all but $21$ steps ($0.026\%$)---the one-step
$1-\delta = 99.9\%$ coverage of \eqref{eq:tubefit} transfers essentially
unchanged through the propagation \eqref{eq:tube}. The bound is loose
(median $e_k / \|\delta_k\| \approx 1.8 \times 10^{3}$ at the planning
horizon), which is inherent to the norm recursion: with
$\|A\|_2 \approx 1.00$ and $\sum_i \|B_i\|_2 \approx 1.24$ the growth
factor $\bar m(u) + c_x$ exceeds one at every step. For \bkmbd{} this
looseness is immaterial---the tube enters as a \emph{relative} penalty
that orders candidates by how far they leave the trusted region, not as a
constraint---but it is why we call the tube empirical and do not attach
certificate semantics to it.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figs/tube_validation.png}
\caption{Empirical tube $e_k$ vs.\ the true lifted error on a
representative held-out FR3 rollout, with the quantile-fitted constants
used by the planner. Over $2{,}000 \times 40$ held-out steps the tube is
violated in $0.026\%$ of steps, matching the fitted $99.9\%$ one-step
coverage.}
\label{fig:tube_val}
\end{figure}

Its fitting, however, must respect the residual
distribution: fitting $(c_x, c_u)$ to the worst-case residual instead of
the quantile bound \eqref{eq:tubefit} lets a handful of rest-to-motion
transients---which a velocity-free lifted state cannot represent---inflate
$c_x$ by $40\times$ (from $0.008$ to $0.32$) on the FR3; the propagated
bound \eqref{eq:tube} then grows exponentially over the horizon and the
tube term---not the task cost---dominates the anneal, leaving \bkmbd{}
oscillating $23$\,cm from a target it otherwise reaches with $2.4$\,cm
accuracy, with no change to the model. The quantile fit
($\delta = 10^{-3}$) restores the intended regularizing role.

\subsection{Delimiting the Regime}\label{subsec:exp_regime}

The bilinear rollout was motivated by first-order coupling; we locate
the boundary of that motivation with two compact studies under the same
training recipe.
On a torque-limited pendulum---a system whose input enters
state-independently, so that Proposition~\ref{prop:const} imposes no
structural penalty---the linear class remains a viable controller:
in the same annealed planner, with a $128$-neuron encoder
it swings up from hanging on $5/5$ planning seeds.
What the bilinear class buys is \emph{compactness}: it attains with a
$32$-neuron encoder the multi-step accuracy the linear model reaches
only at $256$ neurons at the same lifted dimension $r = 8$ (mean over
three training seeds), and the gap carries into closed loop at tighter
budgets, where the width-$64$ linear model fails swing-up ($0/5$) while
the bilinear one already succeeds ($4/5$).
On a $3$D quadrotor in MuJoCo---the canonical platform of the
linear-lift control literature, where thrust reaches the position only
through the attitude---the bilinear model is $2.3$--$2.4\times$ more
accurate on the full feature vector at $H = 12$; on the linear-velocity
rows it is far more accurate at one step (the increment
$\Delta v \propto T\,R e_3$ \emph{is} a state--input product), but over
the horizon both classes become limited by the same attitude-prediction
error and the velocity-row gap settles at $1.5$--$1.8\times$ (three
training seeds).
What remains there is \emph{shared} prediction error, not the
directional input-channel bias of Sec.~\ref{subsec:exp_mechanism}, so
coupling buried behind an integrator leaves a linear lift serviceable
for position control---the regime in which the linear-lift
successes of the Koopman control literature were reported
(Sec.~\ref{sec:intro}).
Together the two studies delimit the claim of this paper: the bilinear
rollout is the repair that first-order coupled outputs demand of a
model-free rollout, an efficiency upgrade where the coupling is absent,
and an optional one where it is buried behind an integrator.

%==============================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
%==============================================================
This paper presented \bkmbd{}, which replaces the simulator rollouts of
Model-Based Diffusion with a learned bilinear deep Koopman surrogate and
a quantile-fitted model-error tube. The motivating representability
argument---the best constant input map is the mean of the
configuration-dependent gain, with an irreducible error equal to its
dispersion---was confirmed by closed-loop experiments
on an SE(2) unicycle, a kinematic $7$-DOF arm, and a physics-simulated
Franka FR3 inside a single planning stack: the full-lift linear rollout
failed or stalled ($0/20$, $0/35$, and $7/35$ strict), while the bilinear
rollout matched the true-dynamics oracle in all $90$ trials and reduced
the FR3 planning step from $4.1$\,s to $64$\,ms, within $1.3\times$ of
the $50$\,ms control period; an optimizer ablation showed the annealed
schedule itself supplies the final precision a single-stage sampler
cannot reach.

The tube constants are empirical coverage proxies rather than certified
bounds in the spirit of~\cite{safedmd2024}, and our tasks are first-order
coupled but contact-free; certified tubes and contact-rich dynamics
remain open. Conversely, where the input enters state-independently or
reaches the output only through an integrator, a linear lift remains a
serviceable controller under the same planner
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:exp_regime}), so the bilinear class is the repair
specifically for first-order coupled outputs. Deployment on the physical
FR3 through the identical joint-velocity interface is the immediate next
step.
